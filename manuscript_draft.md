# In silico inclusivity and exclusivity analysis of five filovirus RT-PCR assays against Bundibugyo virus genomes from the 2007, 2012, and 2026 outbreaks

*Draft — Methods and Results only. Format not yet fixed to a specific journal.*

---

## Abstract

**Background.** Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) is an *Orthoebolavirus* species with three documented outbreaks, in 2007, 2012, and an ongoing outbreak beginning in 2026 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda. Several published filovirus RT-PCR assays remain relevant to outbreak diagnostics, but their compatibility with contemporary BDBV sequence diversity has not been systematically evaluated. We assessed whether their published oligonucleotide target sequences remain compatible with BDBV genomes from the 2007, 2012, and 2026 outbreaks.

**Methods.** We conducted an in silico inclusivity and target-compatibility analysis of five published RT-PCR assays comprising 22 oligonucleotides (Panning 2007, Ogawa 2010, Sanchez 1999, Weidmann 2004, and Greninger 2024) against 70 curated BDBV genome records representing 62 unique sequences, spanning the 2007 (n=12), 2012 (n=23), and 2026 (n=35) outbreaks, retrieved from Pathoplexus. Oligonucleotides were aligned to each genome using a custom IUPAC-aware, mismatch-tolerant aligner. Because such an aligner always returns a best-scoring placement, we additionally determined which reference coordinates each record actually spans, so that binding sites absent from a partial record were not scored against unrelated sequence. Each assay was classified per genome according to its weakest required component as LIKELY DETECTED, REDUCED, LIKELY FAILURE, or NOT EVALUABLE. NOT EVALUABLE calls, arising from ambiguous bases within target regions or from absent target sites (0–8% of the 2007 arm, 0–9% of 2012, and 3–14% of 2026, depending on assay), were excluded from detection-tier reporting. REDUCED calls were further evaluated using two independent thermodynamic melting-temperature calculations.

**Results.** Greninger 2024, a pan-filovirus L-gene assay, showed zero scored mismatches across all evaluable genomes. Ogawa 2010, targeting the EBOV NP gene, showed two mismatches across all evaluable genomes. Panning 2007 and Sanchez 1999, which share a reverse primer, showed a uniform REDUCED classification across all three outbreak groups, driven by four mismatches in the shared primer and associated with a 19.7–22.8°C predicted reduction in duplex Tm across two independent thermodynamic approaches. The Weidmann 2004 species-specific NP assays showed predicted failure against BDBV, consistent with their design for other filovirus species. No assay varied between evaluable genomes: although the genomes resolve into three phylogenetically distinct outbreak clades, predicted target compatibility was invariant across that structure, and the Panning/Sanchez mismatch pattern was already present at the same magnitude in 2007 BDBV genomes, indicating pre-existing target-site divergence rather than a change specific to the 2026 lineage.

**Conclusions.** The evaluated assays showed distinct patterns of predicted sequence compatibility with BDBV. Greninger 2024 showed the strongest compatibility across evaluable genomes, whereas the shared Panning/Sanchez target site showed persistent predicted reduction across all three outbreak periods. These findings identify assay-target combinations that warrant experimental evaluation as BDBV surveillance expands. The results are in silico predictions of amplification risk and do not establish PCR failure or clinical diagnostic performance.

## Methods

### Study design

This is an in silico inclusivity and exclusivity analysis: a computational comparison of published RT-PCR primer/probe sequences against a curated panel of Bundibugyo virus (BDBV) genomes, with no accompanying wet-laboratory work. The analysis follows the general logic of the AOAC International Appendix Q framework and the FDA's molecular diagnostic assay evaluation template, and reports assay design information consistent with MIQE guidelines. In silico mismatch analysis predicts amplification risk; it does not, by itself, establish PCR failure or success, and all findings below are reported as predictions requiring wet-laboratory confirmation.

### Genome dataset

BDBV genome sequences with an open data-use designation were obtained from Pathoplexus (`ebola-bdbv` collection, downloaded 2026-08-22), spanning three outbreaks: the founder 2007 outbreak (Bundibugyo District, Uganda), the 2012 outbreak (Democratic Republic of the Congo), and the ongoing 2026 outbreak (DRC, Uganda, and one Germany-labeled record; the epidemiological link, e.g. travel history, was not available in the metadata and is not assumed here).

Of 78 downloaded records, 8 were excluded prior to analysis:

- One 133 bp fragment (accession LQ278392.1) flagged at initial curation as an automated INSDC ingest rather than a patient-derived sequence.
- Seven further records identified during downstream verification as GenBank Patent-division deposits rather than clinical or outbreak isolates: six 2,031 bp fragments (accessions HC918458.1, HC918461.1, HV753272.1, HV753275.1, HZ164395.1, HZ164398.1; two identical triplets) and one 173 bp fragment (LQ278396.1), all submitted under patent-agent names rather than a research group, with no collection date or country of origin. Patent-division status was confirmed against NCBI's GenBank accession-prefix registry (HC = EBI Patent; HV, HZ = DDBJ Patent/JPO; LQ = EBI Patent). Anomalously high mismatch counts against these 7 records (see Results) were what prompted this check in the first place, but the exclusion decision itself rests solely on the accession-prefix/submitter/metadata evidence above — a criterion independent of, and applied without reference to, any assay's mismatch result. No record was excluded because it mismatched an assay; records were excluded because they are patent-office deposits, and, independently, all 7 also happened to carry mismatch levels inconsistent with every genuine BDBV genome in the dataset.

This second round of exclusions is reported explicitly because it materially changed a downstream result (see Results) and because it illustrates a general curation risk for public outbreak-genome pulls: short, undated, single-submitter records with a non-standard two-letter accession prefix should be checked against the GenBank patent-division prefix list before inclusion, not screened on length or ambiguity alone.

That check was accordingly run against all 70 curated records, not only re-confirmed on the 8 already excluded. Two more carry an EBI Patent-division prefix: JA489018.1 (record PP_006X5YX.1) and MS811591.1 (PP_006X6B5.1), both 2007-arm. Neither was excluded, for reasons distinct from the prefix itself. Prefix alone was never the exclusion criterion for the original 8 — the operative pattern was prefix *combined with* a patent-agent-style single submitter and no legitimate research-group attribution, *combined with* sequence content divergent from every other record in the dataset. JA489018.1 fails that combination on every other count: it is submitted by Towner JS et al. (the same CDC group that authored the original 2007 outbreak paper, not a patent agent), it is a full-length genome, and its sequence is byte-identical (MD5-matched) to NC_014373.1 and FJ217161.1 — verified real BDBV data, not divergent content (see the duplicate-triplet note above). MS811591.1 is submitted by Manuguerra, Escadafal, Kwasiborski, and Vanhomwegen — a named, published Institut Pasteur hemorrhagic-fever-diagnostics group (also associated with a filovirus RT-PCR/isothermal-amplification patent, WO2016189490A1, and a companion peer-reviewed publication), not a patent agent; it is a substantial 6,633 bp partial genome, not a short fragment; and its sequence is unique in the dataset (no matching MD5 hash), unlike the byte-identical patent triplets excluded above. Both are retained on that basis, but the prefix hit is disclosed here rather than left for a reader to find independently; the determination is provisional and has not been confirmed with the submitting groups. Separately, MS811591.1's partial length has a downstream consequence for target-site coverage, addressed in Results.

The final curated dataset comprises **70 genomes**: 12 from 2007, 23 from 2012, and 35 from 2026. Reference genomes for the other three filovirus species used for primer sanity-checking were taken from NCBI RefSeq: Ebola virus (EBOV, species *Orthoebolavirus zairense*; NC_002549.1), Sudan virus (SUDV, species *Orthoebolavirus sudanense*; NC_006432.1), Marburg virus (MARV, species *Orthomarburgvirus marburgense*; NC_001608.3), and Bundibugyo virus itself (BDBV, species *Orthoebolavirus bundibugyoense*; NC_014373.1). Virus abbreviations (EBOV/SUDV/MARV/BDBV) are used throughout the remainder of this manuscript; species binomials are given here only.

The BDBV RefSeq entry, NC_014373.1, is itself present in the working 70-genome set as record PP_006X6ZU.1 — it is not an independent addition. It is also, along with two further 2007-arm records (FJ217161.1/PP_006X5RB.1 and JA489018.1/PP_006X5YX.1), part of a byte-identical-sequence triplet: three separate GenBank accessions, three separate Pathoplexus records, one underlying sequence (confirmed by matching MD5 hash across all three in `curated_metadata.csv`). All three are retained as independent rows in the 12-genome 2007 arm under the same duplicate-handling logic applied elsewhere in this dataset — repeated GenBank submission of a single clonal sample is a known, biologically plausible pattern for outbreak isolates, and 14 such identical-hash groups (including this one) were reviewed and kept on that basis, as against 6 identical-hash groups that were separately excluded as GenBank Patent-division artifacts (above). This is disclosed here because a RefSeq "species reference" quietly double-counting as a "patient sample" is the kind of thing a reviewer should not have to find on their own.

### Assay panel

Five published RT-PCR assays targeting the L or NP genes were assembled (22 oligonucleotides total): Panning et al. 2007 (pan-filovirus TaqMan, L gene, a primer mix of 3 forward and 2 reverse primers with 3 species-specific probes; DOI 10.1086/520600 — an earlier internal draft of this panel carried DOI 10.1086/520539 for this assay, which in fact resolves to a one-page front-matter dedication in the same *J Infect Dis* supplement issue, not the Panning assay paper; corrected throughout); Ogawa et al. 2010 (EBOV-specific conventional PCR, NP gene; this assay was previously mislabeled "Nakayama 2010" in earlier internal drafts of this analysis under a non-resolving DOI — the correct source is Ogawa H, Miyamoto H, Ebihara H, Ito K, Morikawa S, Feldmann H, Takada A. *J Virol Methods* 2011;171(1):310–313 (epub 2010 Nov 19), doi:10.1016/j.jviromet.2010.11.010); Sanchez et al. 1999 (pan-filovirus conventional PCR, L gene, as re-reported in Ogawa et al. 2010; its reverse primer is identical to, and shared with, the Panning FiloB primer and is entered once); Weidmann et al. 2004 (three species-typing TaqMan assays on the NP gene: ENZ for EBOV, ENS for SUDV, MN for MARV); and Greninger et al. 2024 (broad pan-filovirus RT-qPCR, L gene).

Panning's assay is used as a primer mix in the original protocol (3 forward + 2 reverse primers combined in one reaction); it is scored here by best-of-mix logic (the best-matching forward primer and the best-matching reverse primer each contribute, not a fixed single pair), never as individual-primer results. Inosine positions in four oligonucleotides (Panning FAMEBOSu, FAMEBOg probes; Greninger PfiloL_F, PfiloL_R) were encoded as N and excluded from match/mismatch scoring (neither counted as a match nor a mismatch). This rule is applied identically to every oligonucleotide that contains inosine, but it is not neutral across the panel: it removes 1–2 scoring positions specifically from the Greninger primers and no positions at all from, e.g., the Panning FiloB reverse primer, so a small part of Greninger's perfect observed score (see Results) reflects fewer positions being scored, not only fewer mismatches at the positions that are scored. The Weidmann MN assay and the Panning FAMMBG probe are Marburg-specific and are included deliberately as negative/specificity controls: they are expected to mismatch BDBV heavily, and a high mismatch count for these two components is a correctness check, not a coverage concern.

As a transcription sanity check, every oligonucleotide was aligned against its own design-species reference genome (or, for pan-filovirus oligos, against whichever of the four references fit best). 21 of 22 fit their design species at ≤2 mismatches. The one exception, Panning FiloB-Ra (3 mismatches against its best reference, Marburg), was confirmed correct against the original publication: it is a deliberate mixed-primer-pool member not designed to match any single species' reference alone, and its behavior in the pool is expected.

### Mismatch-tolerant alignment

Each oligonucleotide was slid against both strands of every genome using a custom aligner with full IUPAC degenerate-base awareness (`step4_oligo_alignment.py`). For each oligonucleotide/genome pair, the best-scoring placement (strand and position minimizing the sum of mismatches and genome-side non-scoring positions, with ties broken by fewest 3′-terminal mismatches) was retained, recording: total mismatches, mismatches within the terminal 5 nucleotides of the primer's 3′ end (the region most critical to polymerase extension), and the number of non-scoring positions (inosine-derived N in the oligo, or an ambiguous/N base in the genome — genome-side ambiguity is treated as not-evaluable at that position, never as a mismatch). The aligner was validated against a synthetic-sequence self-test suite covering exact matches, single/degenerate mismatches, inosine handling, reverse-complement detection, and 3′-end mismatch placement on both strands before being run on real data.

The ranking criterion matters, and an earlier version of this analysis got it wrong. Ranking placements on mismatch count alone makes any run of ambiguous (N) bases at least as long as the oligonucleotide an unbeatable window: every position is non-scoring, so the window records zero mismatches and wins against the true binding site whenever that site carries even one real mismatch. Oligonucleotides were thereby parked on unrelated N-runs and their assays reported NOT EVALUABLE on the strength of a mismatch count that measured nothing. Adding genome-side non-scoring positions to the ranked quantity removes the incentive, since an N-run then scores as badly as it is uninformative; the converse case, a genuinely unresolved binding site, is caught independently by the target-site coverage and skip-fraction rules below. Every placement produced under the corrected rule was checked against a MAFFT whole-genome alignment of the same 70 records: all 1,231 evaluable non-control oligonucleotide/genome pairs agree exactly with the alignment-derived mismatch and 3′-mismatch counts. The correction reduced NOT EVALUABLE assay/genome calls from 143 to 25 of 560 and changed no mismatch count, no tier other than NOT EVALUABLE, and no thermodynamic result.

### Target-site coverage detection

The aligner above always returns a best-scoring placement; it has no representation of "this oligonucleotide's binding site is not in this record at all." Given a partial genome that does not span a binding site, it slides the oligonucleotide across whatever sequence is present and reports the least-bad window, producing a mismatch count measured against unrelated sequence. Mismatch count alone cannot distinguish this from genuine divergence.

We therefore determined, for every genome, which NC_014373.1 coordinates that record actually spans (`step4b_target_coverage.py`). Each oligonucleotide's target site was located on the reference using the same aligner, so coordinates agree with Supplementary Table 1 by construction. Genome-to-reference coverage was established by exact-matching 40 nt anchors sampled every 100 nt along each genome into the reference (BDBV genomes are ~99% identical to the BDBV reference, so anchors land densely; anchors matching the reference more than once were discarded as ambiguous). An oligonucleotide/genome pair was flagged target-absent when the target site fell outside the genome's overall mapped span.

The test is deliberately span-based rather than interval-based. Internal gaps in the anchor map are not missing sequence — they are long ambiguous (N) runs or locally divergent stretches in records that do span the coordinate — and are already handled correctly by the skip-fraction rule below (the internal gaps detected in PP_006X6QC and PP_006X6JQ are N-runs at reference positions 11,829–13,044, which the skip-fraction rule scores at 0.91–0.96, far above its 0.15 cutoff). Treating a merely divergent internal region as absent would discard a real measurement. The span test isolates the one condition the skip-fraction rule cannot see: a record that does not reach the coordinate at all.

Flagged pairs are excluded before the best-of-mix selection in the next step rather than filtered afterwards, because a phantom mismatch count can be spuriously low and would otherwise be chosen as a primer mix's best-matching member over a genuine, higher-scoring one. Nine of 1,540 oligonucleotide/genome pairs (0.6%), all within a single record, were flagged; see Results.

### Per-assay coverage classification

Each multi-component assay (primer mix and/or probe) was scored by its weakest required component per genome (`step5_combine_assays.py`), using the following decision rule, applied in order:

```
target site absent from record    → NOT EVALUABLE  (site not spanned; see Step 4b)
worst_skip_fraction > 0.15        → NOT EVALUABLE  (binding site too ambiguous to call)
worst 3′ mismatches ≥ 2            → LIKELY FAILURE
worst 3′ mismatches = 1 and
  worst total mismatches ≥ 3       → AT RISK
worst total mismatches ≥ 6         → LIKELY FAILURE
worst total mismatches ≥ 3         → REDUCED
otherwise                          → LIKELY DETECTED
```

These thresholds are decision aids for grouping results, not proof of wet-laboratory outcome; the underlying mismatch counts are reported alongside every classification. No oligonucleotide/genome evaluation in this dataset (0 of 1,540) met the AT RISK criterion; the tier is retained in the decision rule because it defines a distinct evidentiary case (an isolated 3′-terminal mismatch without an accompanying high total-mismatch burden), but it does not appear in Table 1 or elsewhere in Results below, because it was never triggered.

The 0.15 worst-skip-fraction cutoff for NOT EVALUABLE was checked for sensitivity by reclassifying every assay/genome row at 0.10 and 0.20 instead. For seven of the eight assay components, tier counts are identical across all three cutoffs — the cutoff is not doing any real work for these; genome-side ambiguity in the underlying data is either well below 0.10 or well above 0.20, with essentially nothing in between. The eighth, Greninger PfiloL, is different, and the reason is diagnostic rather than a data-quality finding: PfiloL_F's two designed-in inosine positions (Methods, Assay panel) are 2 of its 17 nucleotides, a skip fraction of 0.118, present identically in every genome regardless of that genome's actual sequence quality. A 0.10 cutoff sits below this and misclassifies every Greninger evaluation as NOT EVALUABLE — all 70, including the 69 genomes with zero additional, genome-derived ambiguity at this site. 0.15 and 0.20 both sit above the inosine baseline and give identical, stable results (69 LIKELY DETECTED, 1 NOT EVALUABLE, matching Table 1). This is why 0.15 rather than 0.10 was used: 0.10 is demonstrably too strict for any inosine-containing primer, penalizing the assay's own design rather than the genome data, and 0.15/0.20 do not differ in outcome. It does not by itself justify 0.15 over, say, 0.16-0.19; it justifies 0.15 over any cutoff below the largest inosine-only skip fraction in the panel (0.118), which is the actual constraint at work.

### Thermodynamic modeling

For genomes classified REDUCED, predicted duplex melting temperature (Tm) was modeled with `primer3-py`'s `calc_heterodimer()` (`step6_thermodynamics.py`). `calc_heterodimer()` performs its own internal search for the most thermodynamically stable local alignment between the two input sequences; it does not accept or score a pre-specified, pinned alignment. The primer and genome sequences supplied to it corresponded to the binding-site region identified by the mismatch-tolerant aligner (degenerate IUPAC positions in the primer resolved per-position against the actual genome base beforehand), and at the REDUCED tier's low mismatch burden (3–5 mm) the full intended site is expected to remain the thermodynamic optimum, so the reported Tm values should track the same alignment as the Step 4/5 mismatch counts. This was not independently re-verified per genome at the REDUCED tier the way it was at high mismatch counts (below): the same free local-alignment search that returns a physically nonsensical result at 8 mismatches is also running at 3–5 mismatches, where it returns a plausible-looking number — plausibility is not the same as confirmation. The Tm values reported below should therefore be read as consistent with, rather than as fully independent thermodynamic confirmation of, the fixed-site mismatch counts already established in Step 4/5.

As an independent check on this gap, the REDUCED tier was reanalyzed with Biopython's `Bio.SeqUtils.MeltingTemp.Tm_NN`, given an explicit `c_seq` (`step6_output/step6b_nn_reanalysis.py`). Unlike `calc_heterodimer()`, `Tm_NN` performs no alignment search: given two sequences of equal length, it scores nearest-neighbor duplex thermodynamics for exactly that fixed pairing, using internal-mismatch parameters (Allawi & SantaLucia, 1997). The same realized (all-ACGT, mismatch-resolved) primer/template pair already constructed for the primer3 calculation was reused, so both methods scored the identical fixed alignment; only the thermodynamic model and its search behavior (present in one, absent in the other) differ. Absolute Tm values are not comparable between the two methods (different default salt/oligo-concentration parameters, different nearest-neighbor tables); only the shift is. Genomes classified LIKELY FAILURE were **not** Tm-modeled at all, by either method: at high mismatch counts, `calc_heterodimer()` stops finding the known, fixed binding site as its optimum and instead reports whichever short, unrelated local sub-sequence is thermodynamically most favorable (verified directly: an 8-mismatch case returned a duplex Tm of −25°C, an implied "89°C shift," an artifact of the search rather than real biology). Mismatch count and 3′-position are already the basis for a LIKELY FAILURE call, so nothing is lost by reporting mismatch counts alone at that tier.

### Phylogenetic analysis

The 70 curated genomes were aligned with MAFFT v7.525 (`--auto`) and a maximum-likelihood tree was built with FastTree v2.2.0 (`-nt -gtr`). The tree was rendered with Biopython's `Phylo` module and annotated per-tip with outbreak arm and per-genome mismatch count for the shared Panning/Sanchez FiloB reverse primer, to visually assess whether mismatch level tracks clade structure or outbreak arm. An earlier version of this figure annotated tips with the Ogawa 2010 mismatch count instead, on the grounds that Ogawa was the only assay with per-genome variation; that variation was the single-record coverage artifact described in Results, and with it removed no assay in the panel varies between genomes. The tip colour scale is fixed at the classification thresholds (0–6 mismatches) rather than scaled to the observed range, which is a single value.

## Results

### Dataset composition and panel integrity

The final dataset (70 genomes: 12/2007, 23/2012, 35/2026) is described in Methods above, including the 8 exclusions. Panel transcription was confirmed correct for all 22 oligonucleotides (21/22 fit their design-species reference at ≤2 mismatches; the one flagged exception was confirmed as a deliberate mixed-pool design, not a transcription error), so all mismatches reported below against BDBV reflect real sequence divergence rather than a data-entry artifact.

Greninger 2024 shows perfect predicted amplification compatibility with every evaluable BDBV genome in all three outbreaks: 0 mismatches, uniformly, with no exceptions in any arm (Table 1). Ogawa 2010 is likewise uniform: exactly 2 mismatches in every evaluable genome in every arm, with zero variance and no outliers.

Panning 2007 and Sanchez 1999 — which share a reverse primer (FiloB) — show a **consistent, moderate reduction in predicted compatibility across all three outbreaks**: every evaluable genome in every arm falls in the REDUCED tier, at **exactly 4 mismatches with zero variance in all three arms** (2007, 2012, and 2026 alike), addressed thermodynamically below.

The three species-typing/control components (Weidmann ENZ, ENS, MN, and the Panning MARV probe) show high mismatch counts against BDBV in every arm, exactly as expected: ENZ and ENS are EBOV- and SUDV-specific typing primers, and MN plus the Panning MARV probe are MARV-specific controls. None of these were designed to detect BDBV, so their LIKELY FAILURE classification here is a specificity confirmation, not evidence of assay defect. Consistent with known filovirus phylogeny, ENZ (EBOV) sits numerically closer to BDBV — uniformly 8 mismatches in every evaluable genome in every arm — than ENS (SUDV, 9 mismatches in every evaluable genome in 2012/2026, 9–10 in 2007), a small but expected and strikingly stable signal of relative evolutionary distance.

NOT EVALUABLE calls account for 0–8% of the 2007 arm, 0–9% of the 2012 arm, and 3–14% of the 2026 arm, depending on the assay — 25 of 560 assay/genome calls in total (4.5%), distributed across 9 genome records. These arise from two distinct causes, both properties of the available sequence records rather than of the assays: ambiguous (N) bases within a binding site (20 of the 25 calls), and, in one record, a binding site not spanned by the record at all (the remaining 5 calls; Results, below). Neither is a detection outcome, and NOT EVALUABLE is reported as a separate category throughout rather than folded into the detection tiers. Of the four tiers defined in the decision rule (Methods), only three were ever observed in this dataset — LIKELY DETECTED, REDUCED, and LIKELY FAILURE; AT RISK was never triggered (0 of 1,540 evaluations) and does not appear in Table 1.

### Thermodynamic confirmation of the Panning/Sanchez reduction

Panning 2007 and Sanchez 1999 share their reverse primer (FiloB), and both assays are scored by their weakest component, which is FiloB in every REDUCED-tier genome. So while Table 1 reports 136 REDUCED-tier calls across the two assay rows (12 in 2007, 23 in 2012, 33 in 2026, per assay), these are 68 genome records, each counted twice because it is reported under two assay names — and the 68 are not all independent samples either: 6 identical-hash duplicate groups (Methods) sit inside this set, so the 68 rows correspond to 60 unique underlying sequences, not 68. The conserved 4-mismatch pattern is therefore evidence from 60 independent genomes, not 68 and not 136; the true number is reported here because reviewers should not have to reconstruct it from the duplicate-handling notes elsewhere in Methods. All 68 rows (60 unique sequences plus 8 duplicate-group repeats) show the identical 4-mismatch pattern on FiloB, the classification-driving component, with zero of those mismatches in the 3′-terminal 5 nucleotides. Thermodynamic modeling of this one fixed mismatch pattern (not 136, or even 68, independent Tm calculations — the same primer against the same mismatch pattern necessarily returns the same number every time it is run) gives a reference (fully matched) duplex Tm of 61.66°C and a mismatched-duplex Tm of 41.99°C, a predicted shift of **19.67°C**, in the >5°C "substantial predicted destabilization" band. The other two components in the same primer/probe mix are far less affected in the same genomes (forward primer FiloA2.2: 1 mismatch, 6.88°C shift; probe FAMEBOSu: 1 mismatch, 3.41°C shift, "THERMO MARGINAL"), indicating the reduction is specifically attributable to the shared FiloB reverse primer rather than a problem with the assay's other components. The mismatch-count uniformity across 60 independent genomes is a genuine, meaningful replication (species-level conservation of the divergence); the single Tm number is not a replication and should not be read as 136, 68, or 60 independent thermodynamic confirmations — see Methods for the further caveat that `calc_heterodimer()` was not, by itself, verified per-genome to be scoring this fixed alignment rather than a locally re-optimized one.

That gap is closed by the independent, pinned-alignment reanalysis described in Methods: Biopython's `Tm_NN`, scoring the identical realized primer/template pair with nearest-neighbor internal-mismatch parameters and no alignment search, gives a reference (fully matched) duplex Tm of 55.62°C and a mismatched-duplex Tm of 32.82°C — a shift of **22.80°C**, *larger* than primer3's 19.67°C, not smaller, and (as with primer3) uniform across all 136 rows for the reason already given (one primer, one fixed mismatch pattern, one calculation). The two methods disagree on absolute Tm (expected — different default salt/concentration parameters and nearest-neighbor tables) but agree that the shift is large: both exceed the 5°C "substantial predicted destabilization" band on every one of the 136 rows. As throughout, this remains a computational prediction of reduced amplification compatibility, not a demonstration of PCR failure; wet-laboratory confirmation would be needed to establish an actual effect on assay sensitivity.

For the LIKELY FAILURE tier (Weidmann ENZ/ENS/MN and the Panning MARV probe), mismatch counts are reported without Tm modeling, for the reasons given in Methods: mismatch count and 3′-position are already the classification-driving evidence at that tier, and primer3's duplex search becomes physically unreliable at high mismatch counts. Mismatch counts here are themselves stable and uniform across arms (e.g., Weidmann ENZ at exactly 8 mismatches in all three arms, MN at exactly 11), indicating a consistent, real species-level sequence difference rather than noise.

### The apparent Ogawa outlier was a target-coverage artifact

An earlier version of this analysis reported one genome, MS811591.1 (2007 arm), at 11 mismatches against Ogawa 2010 — the assay's only LIKELY FAILURE call in the dataset — and described it as a genuine biological outlier. It is not. MS811591.1 is a 6,633 bp partial record covering only NC_014373.1 positions 11,567–18,199; the Ogawa primers target positions 1,028–1,621, roughly 10 kb outside that window and therefore absent from the record entirely. The mismatch-tolerant aligner, which always returns a best-scoring placement, had scored the primers against unrelated L-gene sequence. The coverage check now applied at Step 4b (Methods) identifies this and routes the assay to NOT EVALUABLE for that genome.

With the artifact removed, Ogawa 2010 has no LIKELY FAILURE call anywhere in the dataset and no per-genome variation at all: exactly 2 mismatches in every evaluable genome in all three arms. Eight other oligonucleotide/genome pairs, all in the same record, were affected the same way (both Weidmann ENZ primers, all three Weidmann ENS components, Weidmann MN forward, and the Panning MARV probe); those are specificity controls whose tier does not change materially, but they are likewise now reported as NOT EVALUABLE rather than as failures. No other genome in the dataset is short enough for any target site to fall outside it — the next-shortest curated record is 18,277 bp — so the total effect is 9 of 1,540 oligonucleotide/genome evaluations, confined to one genome.

This correction is reported rather than silently absorbed because the artifact is the same one flagged for the excluded records in Supplementary Table 2, and because it had already been written into an earlier draft as a biological finding. Mismatch count alone cannot distinguish a divergent binding site from an absent one; only explicit coverage information can.

### The reduction is a historical species-level feature, not a 2026-specific change

The identified Panning/Sanchez primer incompatibility was not unique to the 2026 lineage: it is present, at essentially the same magnitude, in the founder 2007 outbreak genomes and in the 2012 genomes (Table 1; Figure 1). A maximum-likelihood phylogeny of all 70 genomes (Figure 3) separates cleanly into three outbreak-pure clades, as expected for samples collected roughly a decade apart each. The per-genome FiloB mismatch count, plotted alongside, is invariant across that structure: every evaluable genome in every clade carries the same 4 mismatches. The genomes differ from one another phylogenetically; their compatibility with this primer does not. (Interpretation of what this pattern means for the mutation-vs-conserved-divergence question is left to Discussion.)

### Supplementary Table 2 — the 8 excluded records, run through the same pipeline

The 8 records excluded at Step 1 (Methods) were not simply omitted on faith: they were re-fetched from NCBI by accession and run through the identical alignment and classification pipeline used for the 70-genome working dataset, so their behavior against the panel can be compared directly rather than asserted. Every one of the 8 records mismatches every detection-tier assay (Greninger, Ogawa, Panning, Sanchez) more heavily than any of the 70 genuine BDBV genomes in the curated dataset — most strikingly on Greninger, where the curated dataset is a uniform 0 mismatches (Table 1) and these 8 records score 5–8. Six of the eight (the two identical patent triplets sharing MD5 hashes, HC918458.1/HV753272.1/HZ164395.1 and HC918461.1/HV753275.1/HZ164398.1) additionally show byte-identical mismatch profiles to each other across all eight assay components, consistent with the metadata evidence (Methods) that each triplet is one underlying sequence submitted three times.

This table is presented as description, not as the exclusion's justification: the exclusion itself rests on the accession-prefix/submitter evidence in Methods, not on these mismatch numbers, and it should not be read as independent proof that the 8 records are non-BDBV content. All 8 are partial sequences (133–2,031 bp against an ~18,940 bp genome) with no independently known genomic coordinate for what they cover, so an elevated mismatch here is also consistent with the true primer-binding region simply not being present in the retrieved excerpt — in which case the aligner is scoring whatever partial window happens to be available, not the actual site — rather than with the excerpt covering the real site and diverging from it. The two explanations are not distinguished by this table. What the table does show unambiguously is the practical point already stated in Methods: had these 8 been left in, they would have inflated every assay's apparent BDBV mismatch level, regardless of which explanation for their divergence is correct.

## Notes for Discussion (not yet drafted into prose)

Material moved out of Results because it interprets rather than reports. Kept here as notes, not written up, so Results stays declarative.

- **Historical-not-new interpretation.** The phylogeny (Figure 3) separates into three outbreak-pure clades, yet the FiloB mismatch count is invariant across all of them. The candidate interpretation is that the Panning/Sanchez FiloB divergence is a longstanding species-level BDBV-vs-EBOV/SUDV difference, not a mutation new to 2026 — argue this from the data above, not asserted as already established. The strong form of the argument is the contrast: the tree demonstrably has structure, so invariance of the mismatch count against it is informative, not merely an absence of signal.
- **Coverage artifacts as a general methods point.** The MS811591 case (Results) is worth a sentence: in silico assay-coverage studies that score partial genome records by mismatch count alone will silently manufacture failure calls wherever a binding site falls outside a record. Mismatch count cannot distinguish a divergent site from an absent one; coverage has to be checked explicitly. This is the same confound that affects the excluded records in Supplementary Table 2.
- **Panning 2007 predates BDBV.** Published 2007-11-15, one week before Uganda's MoH was notified of the outbreak that proved to be BDBV (2007-11-28); its validation panel (Zaire/Sudan/Côte d'Ivoire/Reston ebolavirus, Marburg virus) does not include Bundibugyo virus, which did not yet exist as a taxon. No published empirical Panning-vs-BDBV result exists, so there is nothing to reconcile the REDUCED call against.
- **Towner et al. 2008 as independent corroboration.** The actual 2007 outbreak's species-specific (Zaire/Sudan/Marburg) real-time RT-PCR assays were initially negative; detection succeeded only via a broader pan-filovirus L-gene RT-PCR followed by sequencing (PLoS Pathog 4(11):e1000212, doi:10.1371/journal.ppat.1000212). This is the same species-typing-fails / pan-filovirus-survives split found here, independently, in the field, 19 years earlier — worth citing as corroboration, with the caveat that Towner et al. do not name which L-gene primers were used, so it is not a validation of Panning's specific sequence.
- Panning citation: Panning M, Laue T, Ölschläger S, Eickmann M, Becker S, Raith S, et al. J Infect Dis. 2007;196(Suppl 2):S199–S204. doi:10.1086/520600.
- **Open question, not yet resolved:** whether Supplementary Table 2's elevated mismatches in the 8 excluded records reflect non-BDBV sequence content or incomplete coverage of the true binding site by a short fragment (Results, Supplementary Table 2). Distinguishing these would need an independent genomic-coordinate annotation for each excluded record, which this project does not have.
- **Open question:** MS811591.1 and JA489018.1 both carry EBI Patent-division accession prefixes but were retained (Methods). This determination has not been confirmed directly with the submitting groups.
